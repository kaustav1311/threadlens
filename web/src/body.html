The compatibility test for people who argue

Who actually started it?

Six questions, one chat export, no opinions. Threadlens counts what each of you actually did — who asked, who asserted, who sourced it, who changed the subject, whose temper moved first — and applies the exact same ruler to both. It will not tell you who was right. It will make that argument a great deal harder to keep having.

Works on WhatsApp, Instagram, Messenger, Twitter / X, Discord, Reddit — an export, or just the messages copied off the screen.

Six questions, one report

One page, read top to bottom. Every section is the same numbers counted the same way for each of you — and a section that this chat cannot answer does not appear at all.

Who actually started it?

Your chat is split into conversations at six-hour gaps, and each one has a person who opened it. Alongside that: share of messages, words per message, median reply time and the hours you each keep. The baseline everything else sits on.

Do you even speak the same language?

Literally. It works out whether a chat is English, romanised Bengali, romanised Hindi or a mix, and treats each one's function words as function words. Then it compares how much of each other's vocabulary you actually pick up — and how often one of you answers in a register the other never uses.

Which of you brings receipts?

Every factual-sounding sentence goes in a ledger with whether it pointed at anything checkable: a link, a named report, a date, a number. Threadlens never marks a claim true or false. It tells you which ones are worth going and checking.

Who actually answers the question?

Questions come in three kinds and only one is a debt. A check-in like Wbu? wants acknowledgement; a whataboutism wants a point scored; a real question wants an answer. Only the last kind can go unanswered, which is why this number is finally believable.

Whose temper turns up first?

A 0–1 heat trend per person over time, absolutist words, group labels, status put-downs and profanity — plus moral vocabulary, which is often why you are talking past each other: one of you is arguing from fairness and the other from loyalty.

Are you even arguing about the same thing?

Drift from what each conversation opened on, and the moments someone changed the subject right after being challenged. This section only appears when your chat actually contains an argument — on three years of small talk it says so instead of scoring one.

How it works

  1. Get the chat out

    WhatsApp → the chat → More → Export chat → Without media. Instagram, Messenger, Twitter / X and Discord all have a data export too — or just select the messages and copy them.

  2. Drop it in

    Android or iPhone, 12- or 24-hour, .txt, .zip or a Word copy. Timestamps are optional: pasted messages are read in order. It never leaves this tab.

  3. Read the answer

    It finds the arguments, scores those and leaves the small talk alone. Open any number to see exactly how it was counted.

What is the scoring actually doing?

Counting, carefully, and telling you so. Word lists, phrase patterns and a few small formulas — no black box and no model deciding what you meant. The full method, every weight and every threshold is written up in docs/SCORING.md, and the word lists are a single readable JSON file you can send a pull request against.

“Compatibility test” — does it score our compatibility?

No, and it never will. There is no compatibility number anywhere in this tool, and adding one is the single change it is built to make difficult. What it does is answer the six questions in the headline with counts, side by side, using the same ruler for each of you — who opened, who sourced, who answered, who changed the subject. Whether that adds up to compatible is a judgement, and it is yours. The satire is in the framing. The numbers underneath are deadly literal.

Can it tell me if my relationship is in trouble?

No, and be suspicious of anything that says it can. It can show you that one of you starts 80% of the conversations, or that the warmth fell off a cliff in March, or that neither of you has asked the other a question in six weeks. Those are worth a conversation. They are not a diagnosis, and a chat log is a thin slice of a relationship.

Does it work if we type in two languages at once?

Better than it used to. It works out which languages a chat is actually written in — English, romanised Bengali, romanised Hindi — and treats each one's function words as function words. Before that, ami, kore and theke read as rare, distinctive terms, which meant the “topic” of a conversation came out as noise and half of what looked like an unanswered question was Wbu?. Tone and heat still undercount non-English, because the sentiment lexicon is English.

What will it get wrong?

Sarcasm, every time. Quoting someone else's insult counts against you. Tone and heat undercount every language the sentiment lexicon does not cover. It can tell an assertion from a plan or a piece of advice most of the time, but not an assertion from a strongly-worded opinion — it was violence for a political cause is a claim, it is political in every way is not, and a word list cannot see the difference. Comparisons switch off below 150 words per person. Read the messages, not just the bars.

Is there a command-line version?

Yes, and an optional self-hosted API, both offline. pip install threadlens, then threadlens analyse chat.zip --lens rigor --ledger claims.json exports the claim ledger for you — or an LLM — to fact-check. Local ML models (toxicity, fallacy classification, self-contradiction detection) live behind the [deep] extra.

Which open-source parts is this built on?

All of them. Tone uses the VADER lexicon (MIT, Hutto & Gilbert); the absolutist word list follows Al-Mosaiwi & Johnstone; the moral vocabulary follows Moral Foundations Theory; everything else is an original list in one readable JSON file. The page itself has no dependencies at all beyond a zip reader, which is why it fits in a single file you can save and run offline. Run it deeper on your own machine and the [deep] extra adds Detoxify, an open fallacy classifier and a local NLI model for self-contradiction — or point it at a model you already run locally. Nothing is ever sent anywhere, and no model gets a vote in the web app's numbers.

Where your chat goes

Nowhere. That is the whole design, and it is enforced rather than promised.

How can I be sure?

This page ships a Content-Security-Policy of connect-src 'none', so the browser refuses to let it open a network connection at all — even if the code tried. Open your network tab and watch nothing happen. Or save the page and run it with the Wi-Fi off; it behaves identically. There is no server, no account, no cookie and no analytics, and a test in the repo fails the build if anyone adds one.

Should I be analysing someone else's messages?

A chat you are in is also theirs. Use “Hide names” before you screenshot anything, keep reports to yourself, and do not publish another person's messages because a chart made them look bad. Depending on where you live, publishing them may also be their decision to make, not yours.

Any limits?

25 MB, 250,000 messages, and 8 analyses per 10 minutes — the last one purely so a huge export cannot lock up your own browser. No server means nothing else needs limiting.
